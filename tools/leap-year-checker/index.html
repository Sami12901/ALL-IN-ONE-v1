Why Do Leap Years Exist? The Astronomical Solar Year

It takes the Earth approximately 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes, and 45.25 seconds (365.24219 solar days) to complete one orbit around the Sun.

If our calendar year remained strictly 365 days, our calendar would drift by about 6 hours each year, shifting the seasons by roughly 24 days every century. Julius Caesar introduced the Julian Calendar (adding 1 day every 4 years), but that assumed a solar year of exactly 365.25 days, overcorrecting by 11 minutes per year.

By 1582, the calendar had drifted by 10 whole days. Pope Gregory XIII introduced the Gregorian Reform: century years (divisible by 100) are only leap years if they are also divisible by 400. This brings the average calendar year to 365.2425 days, accurate to within 1 day every 3,030 years!
