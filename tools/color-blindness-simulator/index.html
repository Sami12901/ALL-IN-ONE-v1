Normal Trichromacy
Standard Human Vision (L, M, S Cones)
Reference Baseline
Full trichromatic perception with standard operational L (red), M (green), and S (blue) photoreceptor cones.
Protanopia / Protanomaly
Red-Blind • L-Cone Deficient
~1.3% Males • 0.02% Females
Long-wavelength (red) cone photoreceptors are missing or anomalous. Reds appear dark, brownish, or indistinguishable from olive greens.
Deuteranopia / Deuteranomaly
Green-Blind • M-Cone Deficient
~5.0% Males • 0.4% Females
Medium-wavelength (green) cone photoreceptors are missing or altered. Most common CVD type; difficulty distinguishing reds, yellows, and greens.
Tritanopia / Tritanomaly
Blue-Blind • S-Cone Deficient
~0.01% Population (Rare)
Short-wavelength (blue) cone photoreceptors are missing. Blues look greenish, yellows look pinkish or violet.
Achromatopsia
Complete Color Blindness • Rod Vision
~0.003% Population
Complete absence of functional cone cells; vision is mediated solely by rod photoreceptors in shades of gray based on luminance.

Designing for Color Vision Deficiency (WCAG & Usability)

Approximately 1 in 12 men (8%) and 1 in 200 women (0.5%) worldwide have some degree of color vision deficiency. Color alone should never be the sole indicator for conveying critical state, errors, or navigation cues.

1. Double Encoding

Combine color cues with icons, shapes, or visible textual labels so meaning is clear even when colors blur together.

2. Contrast Ratio

Ensure WCAG AA contrast of at least 4.5:1 for body copy and 3:1 for large typography and active UI controls against backgrounds.

3. Dangerous Pairings

Avoid red/green and blue/purple adjacent pairs in dashboards, charts, and buttons without distinct luminance or boundary borders.

4. Pattern & Hatching

For data visualizations, bar charts, and heatmaps, use dotted, striped, or textured fills alongside color codes.
